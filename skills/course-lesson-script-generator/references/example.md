**[HOOK — 0:00–0:45]**

You've tried to budget before. You set everything up on a Sunday night, you felt really good about it, and by the 12th of the month it was completely off the rails. Sound familiar?

Here's what went wrong — and it wasn't a willpower problem. The budget you made was designed to fail. Most first budgets are. They're either so detailed you can't maintain them, or so vague they don't actually tell you what to do when you're standing in a grocery store deciding whether to buy the fancy pasta.

This lesson fixes that. I'm going to give you a budgeting method that works specifically for people who have tried and quit before — not because it's perfect, but because it's honest about how you actually spend money.

---

**[OBJECTIVES FRAME — 0:45–1:30]**

By the end of this lesson, you're going to walk away with two things:

First, a complete first-month budget built on the zero-based method — every dollar assigned, nothing left floating. You don't need to have it perfect. You need to have it done.

Second, you're going to identify the one spending category in your life that blows up every budget you've ever made. Not five categories. One. Because fixing one thing completely is worth more than partially fixing five.

This matters because most people fail at budgeting not because they can't do math, but because they don't know where their money is actually leaking. We're going to find your leak today.

---

**[SECTION 1: The Zero-Based Method — 1:30–4:00]**

You've probably heard of the 50/30/20 rule — 50% of your income to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings. It's a nice framework. Here's the problem: it's too vague for someone just starting out.

If you make $3,800 a month after taxes and 30% is "wants," you have $1,140 for wants. What does that mean? Is a streaming subscription a want? Is a work lunch a want? The 50/30/20 rule doesn't help you decide.

Zero-based budgeting is different. The rule is simple: your income minus your expenses equals zero. Every single dollar you earn gets assigned a job before the month starts. The dollar goes to rent, or groceries, or your emergency fund, or your credit card — but it doesn't just float in your checking account waiting to get spent on something you'll regret.

Here's how to build one:

**Step 1:** Write down your total take-home income for the month. One number. If it varies, use the lowest of the last three months.

**Step 2:** List your fixed expenses first — things that don't change. Rent, car payment, insurance, subscriptions. Write the exact dollar amount next to each one.

**Step 3:** List your variable expenses — groceries, gas, eating out, entertainment. This is where you estimate. For your first budget, use what you actually spent last month, not what you wish you'd spent.

**Step 4:** Add a savings or debt payment line. Even $50 counts. This gets assigned just like any other expense.

**Step 5:** Subtract everything from your income. If you hit zero, great. If you're negative, you have to make a cut somewhere. If you're positive, you assign the extra — don't leave it unallocated.

That's the whole method. The power is that every dollar has a job. When you're standing in Target wondering if you should buy something, you can check the category and know immediately whether the money is there.

---

**[SECTION 2: Finding Your One Leak — 4:00–6:30]**

Here's the thing about first budgets: almost everyone has one category that wrecks them. One. Not five. One.

For some people, it's eating out. They budget $200 for restaurants, spend $400, and their entire budget is gone. For others, it's Amazon or Target runs — they show up for paper towels and leave $80 poorer. For others, it's their grocery bill, which is always twice what they planned.

Your job right now is to figure out which one is yours.

Go look at your last two months of bank or credit card statements. Don't analyze everything — just scan for the one category where your actual spending was most different from what you would have guessed.

That category is your leak.

Once you find it, do something counterintuitive: over-allocate to it in your first budget. If you think you spend $200 on eating out, budget $300. Give yourself room to be human. You're not trying to fix the leak this month — you're trying to see it clearly.

**Engagement check:** Before you move on, write down your one leak category. Not in your head — write it down, or type it somewhere. This is the category you're going to watch for the next 30 days.

---

**[SECTION 3: Making It Stick — 6:30–8:30]**

The last thing I want to talk about is what to do when the budget breaks — because it will break, at least a little.

The mistake most people make is treating a blown category like a failed budget. They go over on groceries by $40, decide the whole thing is ruined, and stop tracking. That's like getting a flat tire and deciding to slash the other three.

When you go over in a category, you do one thing: you steal from another category. If you spent $40 more on groceries than you budgeted, you take $40 from your "eating out" category, or your "fun money" category. The total still equals zero. The budget still works. You just had to adjust.

This is called rolling with the punches, and it's how real budgeters actually operate. The budget isn't a law. It's a plan. Plans get adjusted.

Two practical tools that help:

First, check your budget mid-month. Not once a month at the end — mid-month, when you can still make adjustments. Set a phone reminder for the 15th.

Second, have a "miscellaneous" category in your first budget. Budget $50 for it. This is your catch-all for things you forgot to plan for. It won't cover everything, but it reduces the friction of having to steal from another category for small surprises.

---

**[LESSON BRIDGE — 8:30–9:30]**

You now have the framework for your first real budget. Zero-based, one job per dollar, one leak identified, and a plan for what to do when things go sideways.

Here's your homework before the next lesson: build the budget. Use a spreadsheet, a piece of paper, or a free tool like YNAB or EveryDollar — doesn't matter which. Just get the numbers down before we meet again.

Because in the next lesson, we're going to talk about what happens after you have the budget: how to track your spending during the month without it becoming a second job, and the one habit that separates people who stick with budgeting from everyone who quits.

See you there.
