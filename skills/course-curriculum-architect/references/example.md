### Detailed Lesson Outline — Lesson 2.2: The Histogram

```
LESSON 2.2: The Histogram — Your Cheat Sheet for Perfect Exposure
Module: Reading Light Like a Photographer
Duration: 12 minutes
Format: Video (screen + camera demo) + downloadable histogram reference card

LEARNING OBJECTIVE:
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to read a histogram
and use it to determine whether a photo is underexposed, overexposed,
or correctly exposed.

CONTEXT (1 min):
Your camera's screen lies to you. It looks different in bright sunlight
vs a dark room. The histogram doesn't lie — it shows you the actual
distribution of light in your photo, regardless of screen brightness.
Once you learn to read it, you'll stop guessing and start knowing.

CORE CONTENT (8 min):
- What the histogram is: a graph of brightness values from pure black
  (left) to pure white (right)
- Show three example photos with their histograms:
  1. Underexposed — histogram bunched on the left, shadow clipping
  2. Overexposed — histogram bunched on the right, highlight clipping
  3. Well-exposed — histogram spread across the full range
- Demonstrate on-camera: how to turn on histogram display (show for
  Canon, Nikon, and Sony menus)
- Shoot the same scene three times with different exposure, show how
  the histogram changes in real time
- Address the myth: "a good histogram is always a bell curve" — not
  true. A night scene should be left-heavy. A snow scene should be
  right-heavy. The histogram should match the scene.

ACTIVITY (3 min):
Take three photos of the same subject: one deliberately underexposed,
one deliberately overexposed, and one that you think is correct. Check
the histogram for each. Write one sentence describing what the histogram
told you about each shot.

KEY TAKEAWAY:
The histogram is the only honest feedback your camera gives you — learn
to glance at it after every shot and you'll catch exposure mistakes
before they ruin a session.
```

## Key Patterns Across All Three Niches

**1. Every course starts with a quick win.**
Module 1 in each curriculum gives the student something tangible within the first lesson or two. Mike's students take a manual-mode photo. Priya's students identify their quitting pattern and get an accountability partner. Jordan's students have a live portfolio. Practical first activities give learners something concrete to try; effects on continuation and refunds need measurement.

**2. Module count stays between 4 and 6.**
None of these courses have 12 modules. Module count alone does not establish a completion rate. If you have more material than fits in 6 modules, save it for an advanced follow-up course.

**3. Every lesson has an activity.**
Not "think about this" — a specific task with a specific output. Courses without activities are just YouTube playlists someone is charging for. The activities are what make the course worth the price.

**4. Pricing ties to the student's context, not the creator's effort.**
Mike's photography course is $97 because hobbyists spend discretionary income on a hobby. Jordan's proposed freelancing course price is $249; the learning goal does not guarantee student income. The amount of work to build each course is roughly similar, but the price is set by what the student gains.

**5. The beta cohort approach works for all three.**
Every example recommends pre-selling to a small group at a discount before building the full course. A small beta can test demand and reveal curriculum issues before a larger build. Sales, helpful feedback and approved testimonials are possible observations to collect, not guaranteed outputs of the approach.

**6. Launch strategy depends on where the audience lives.**
Possible tests: Mike could explain the course to his YouTube audience; Jordan could draft a permission-based email sequence; Priya could compare interest from her supplied channels. These are proposals, not established conversion paths. Use only real offer deadlines and measure interest before committing to a full launch.
