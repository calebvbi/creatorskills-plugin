```
I make calisthenics content on YouTube. My whole thing is helping skinny guys who've

months in. I've got 50K YouTube subscribers and post every Tuesday. My vibe is
direct and no-BS. No supplements, no gym memberships, no fluff.

Platforms: YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Twitter/X, LinkedIn
```

---

### Consistency Notes
Across all platforms, the brand centers on one concrete transformation story — 130 lbs to muscle-ups in 18 months — because it's the most credible version of the creator's authority. The "no gym, no supplements, no BS" positioning is stated or implied on every platform but adapted to fit each context: explicit on TikTok and Instagram where space is tight and personality carries the weight, more developed on LinkedIn and YouTube where the audience needs context before they trust. LinkedIn skips the "no BS" tone slightly and adds collaboration framing, which is appropriate for the professional context.

---

### Why This Works

**The origin story does the heavy lifting.** "130 lbs → muscle-ups in 18 months" is a specific, verifiable transformation that every skinny beginner can project themselves into. It beats "fitness creator with 50K subs" because it answers the question a potential follower actually asks: "Has this person been where I am?" The numbers give it credibility. The timeframe makes it feel achievable, not intimidating.

**Platform formatting is doing real work here, not just window dressing.** The Instagram bio uses line breaks to create visual rhythm — each line is a short statement that lands before the reader moves to the next. That vertical reading pattern is how Instagram profiles are scanned. The TikTok bio puts everything in one sentence because TikTok bios are read in about 1.5 seconds before the person taps to watch a video. They're not looking for your life story.

**The LinkedIn bio pivots the audience correctly.** LinkedIn readers aren't potential fans — they're potential collaborators, sponsors, and brand partners. So the LinkedIn summary explains the same story from a professional angle: here's the channel, here's the audience, here's why they'd want to reach them. "Feel free to reach out" is a deliberate CTA that signals openness to partnership without sounding desperate.

**The no-fluff positioning creates instant differentiation.** In the fitness niche, the most common bio language is aspirational and vague: "helping you build your best body," "your fitness journey starts here." This creator's "no gym. no supplements. just work." is immediately distinguishable — and it pre-qualifies followers. People who want supplement recommendations won't follow. People who've been turned off by the gym industrial complex will.

**Character counts, verified:**
- Instagram: 126/150 — readable with room for line breaks; cramming to 150 would hurt visual rhythm
- TikTok: 75/80 — punchy, complete thought, five chars to spare
- Twitter: 135/160 — no padding, nothing wasted

### Consistency Notes
This creator's brand is built almost entirely on one differentiator — the 90-day testing standard — and the bios reflect that across every platform. On Twitter and LinkedIn, it's stated explicitly because those audiences care about credibility signals. On TikTok and Instagram, it's compressed into "used for months" and "no fluff" because space is limited and personality carries more weight there. The dry, slightly sarcastic tone shows up most in the LinkedIn summary ("an embarrassing amount of money testing") which is deliberate — it signals that this creator maintains their voice even in professional contexts, which builds trust with brand partners who've been burned by creators who perform professionalism and then go rogue.

---

### Why This Works

**One differentiator, stated every way possible.** "90 days before reviewing anything" is this creator's entire value proposition — it's what separates them from every tech YouTube channel doing 72-hour reviews before dropping an affiliate link. The bios say it four different ways depending on the platform: "used for months before I talk about them" (Instagram), "try every productivity app so you don't have to" (TikTok, implied), "90 days before reviewing them" (Twitter, explicit), "90 days minimum" (LinkedIn, formal). Same truth, different registers.

**"No fluff" as a positioning statement does double duty.** It describes the creator's style AND signals who the audience is. Remote workers and freelancers who resonate with "no fluff" are the people who've wasted money on hyped tools that didn't survive real use. The language itself acts as a filter: people who want production value and enthusiastic unboxings will scroll past. The right audience will stop.

**The Instagram structure earns its line breaks.** Each line in the Instagram bio makes a standalone claim:
- "Productivity apps, reviewed honestly." — The what
- "Used for months before I talk about them." — The differentiator
- "For remote workers who hate fluff." — The who
- "Tool list ↓" — The CTA

This works better than a wall of text because each line loads quickly on mobile. Readers don't even have to finish the bio before they know if this is for them.

**LinkedIn uses personality as a trust signal.** Most LinkedIn summaries in the creator/influencer space fall into one of two traps: either stiff corporate language ("leveraging my platform to deliver value") or over-casual language that feels unprofessional. This summary threads the needle — "an embarrassing amount of money testing productivity apps" is honest, slightly self-deprecating, and signals that this person has actual skin in the game. Brand managers reading that summary immediately understand: this creator has a voice, it's consistent, and their audience trusts them because of it.

**TikTok bio leads with the audience benefit, not the creator's credentials.** "I try every productivity app so you don't have to" — that's a promise of value to the viewer before the creator even mentions themselves. Compare to the generic version: "Productivity creator | Remote work tips | App reviews." One of those makes you stop scrolling. The other is wallpaper.

## Key Takeaways From Both Examples

1. **One specific detail beats ten adjectives.** "130 lbs to muscle-ups in 18 months" is more persuasive than "results-driven fitness creator." "90 days minimum before I review anything" is more persuasive than "honest, thorough reviews." Find the one concrete thing that proves your value and build the bios around it.

2. **Platform character limits are constraints, not annoyances.** The 80-character TikTok limit forces you to figure out what actually matters most. The 150-character Instagram limit forces prioritization. Creators who fight these limits by cram everything in produce bios nobody reads. The skill treats them as design constraints that produce better work.

3. **Audience specificity is a feature, not a risk.** Both bios explicitly narrow their audience — "skinny beginners" and "remote workers who hate fluff." Narrowing your stated audience doesn't reduce your potential followers; it makes the right followers feel found. Someone who is a skinny beginner reads "calisthenics for skinny beginners" and thinks this channel was made for them. That's more powerful than a bio designed to appeal to everyone.

4. **Consistency doesn't mean repetition.** Both bio suites maintain a consistent brand across five platforms without saying the exact same thing on each one. LinkedIn gets the professional framing. TikTok gets the one-liner. YouTube gets the full context. The voice is the same. The packaging is different.

5. **The LinkedIn bio is for a different reader.** On Instagram and TikTok you're writing for potential fans. On LinkedIn, you're writing for potential collaborators, brand partners, and sponsors. The same creator information needs different framing for each audience — the skill handles this automatically when you tell it all your platforms at once.
