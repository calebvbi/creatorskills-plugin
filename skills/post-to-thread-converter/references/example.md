## Example 1: Blog Post — Productivity and Time Management

### Source Content (Abbreviated)

The creator pasted this blog post excerpt — roughly 350 words from a longer piece:

---

*"Why I Stopped Using a To-Do List (And What I Do Instead)"*

I had 47 items on my to-do list the day I decided to delete the app.

The list had been growing for eight months. Some tasks had been on it so long they felt like furniture — permanent fixtures I scrolled past every morning without actually seeing them. "Research podcast equipment." "Reply to Jason." "Fix homepage copy." They'd been there since March.

The problem with a to-do list isn't that it doesn't work. It's that it works too well at one thing: making you feel like you're managing your work when you're actually just cataloging it.

Here's what I switched to instead: every Sunday night, I write down exactly three things I need to accomplish in the coming week. Not three categories. Three specific outcomes. "Record and publish episode 47." "Send the course outline to Sarah for review." "Finalize the sponsor brief for Q2."

That's the entire list.

What I found in the first month: I was getting my three things done, every single week, consistently for the first time in years. But here's what surprised me more — my overall output went up, not down. I was producing more with a three-item list than I had been with a 47-item list.

The reason took me a while to figure out. When everything is a priority, nothing is. The 47-item list wasn't helping me be productive — it was diffusing my attention so thinly that I was making progress on everything and finishing nothing. The three-item list forced prioritization at the planning stage, which meant I spent less time each day deciding what to work on and more time actually working.

I've now been doing this for eleven months. I've published 40+ podcast episodes, launched a course, and grown my email list from 800 to 6,200 subscribers — all while working roughly 30 hours a week.

The to-do list wasn't the problem. The length was.

### Why This Works

**The hook tweet earns its attention.** "I deleted my 47-item to-do list eight months ago. My output went up." delivers the counterintuitive conclusion first — the thing that makes a reader stop and think "wait, what?" The number "47" is specific enough to feel real and relatable. The thread then spends the next 8 tweets paying off that claim.

**The thread tells a story, not a list.** This is not "7 tips for better productivity." It's a specific thing that happened to a specific person, with a clear before/after and a mechanism that explains why. The reader moves through tweets 1-8 feeling like they're getting closer to understanding something, not just receiving facts.

**Tweet 3 uses specificity to create emotion.** "They felt like furniture. I scrolled past them every morning without actually seeing them. That's not a productivity system. That's a graveyard with checkboxes." — the last sentence is the kind of line that gets screenshotted. It's in the original source content, just lightly sharpened for Twitter format.

**The LinkedIn post finds a different angle.** The thread leads with the result and builds toward the mechanism. The LinkedIn post leads with a more personal, observed insight — "they don't fail because they're disorganized, they fail because they're too good at one thing" — which is a more conversational observation that lands better in LinkedIn's slower, more thoughtful feed.

**The LinkedIn opener passes the preview test.** The first line — "I had 47 items on my to-do list the day I deleted the app." — cuts off naturally at the "See more" button with a cliffhanger. The reader has to click through to find out what happened.

**Both formats keep the creator's voice.** The original uses first-person, informal language, and specific personal details. The repurposed content preserves all of it — the specific number "47", the specific subscriber counts, the Sunday night ritual. Nothing was genericized.

## Example 2: YouTube Transcript — Cooking and Recipe Content

### Source Content (Abbreviated)

The creator pasted a rough transcript excerpt — about 300 words from a 12-minute video on weeknight cooking:

---

*[Transcript excerpt from "Why I Stopped Meal Prepping (And What Actually Saves Me More Time)"]*

...so I meal prepped religiously for about two years. Every Sunday. Hours of chopping, cooking, portioning. I had the containers, the system, the whole thing. And I was always kind of proud of it, right? Like it felt responsible.

But then I started actually tracking how much of what I prepped was getting eaten versus thrown away, and the number was rough. Somewhere between 30 and 40 percent of what I made on Sunday was in the trash by Thursday. Because by Thursday I was tired of it. Or my plans changed. Or it just didn't sound good anymore.

The problem with meal prep isn't the prep — it's the commitment. You're essentially deciding on Sunday what you'll want to eat on Wednesday, which is a weird thing to do when you think about it. Taste preferences are emotional, they're contextual, they change.

What I do now instead is what I call "ingredient prep." I don't cook meals in advance. I prep components. I roast a sheet pan of vegetables — no seasoning, no sauce, just roasted. I cook a big pot of grains. I hard-boil a dozen eggs. I wash and dry all my greens.

None of it is a meal. All of it becomes a meal in 10 minutes.

The difference is that I'm not committing to eating the same thing five times. I'm just giving future-me a head start. Roasted vegetables go in a grain bowl Monday. They go on a flatbread Tuesday. They go in eggs Wednesday. Different meal every night, 10-minute assembly each time.

I've been doing this for 14 months. I throw away almost nothing...

### Why This Works

**The hook tweet uses a confession to create credibility.** "I meal prepped every Sunday for 2 years. I was throwing away 30-40% of it by Thursday." — this is someone who tried the thing and found the flaw in it. That's a more credible position than someone who never tried meal prep saying it doesn't work. The specificity of "30-40%" and "2 years" grounds the claim immediately.

**Tweet 2 does the analytical work in one move.** "You're deciding on Sunday what you'll want to eat on Wednesday. That's a weird thing to do." — this reframes a practice millions of people do without question. That reframe is the intellectual engine of the entire thread. Putting it in tweet 2 means anyone who reads past the hook gets the key insight immediately.

**The thread shifts tone at tweet 4.** Tweets 1-3 are the problem. Tweet 4 — "What I switched to: ingredient prep." — is a hard pivot that creates forward momentum. Readers know they're about to get the answer. This structural shift keeps engagement up in the middle of the thread, where most threads lose people.

**The list in tweet 5 is earned, not lazy.** The bullet-point format in tweet 5 works because it's specific and concrete ("no seasoning, no sauce — just roasted"), not vague ("cook some vegetables, prepare some proteins"). The reader can immediately visualize the Sunday routine.

**The LinkedIn post finds the emotional core.** Where the thread leads with the data point (30-40% waste), the LinkedIn post leads with the feeling — "The containers were full. So was the trash by Thursday." That sentence is blunter and more visual. It resonates faster on a platform where people are scrolling between job announcements and industry think-pieces.

**"Optionality" is the phrase worth preserving.** The original transcript used informal language throughout. The phrase "prep optionality" was the creator's own framing, and it's good enough to anchor both the thread and the LinkedIn post. Keeping the creator's actual vocabulary makes the content feel more authentic than a paraphrase.

## Key Takeaways From Both Examples

1. **The angle matters more than the topic.** "Productivity" and "meal prep" are topics. "I deleted my 47-item to-do list and my output went up" and "I was throwing away a third of my meal prep for two years" are angles. The angle is specific, surprising, and earns attention. The skill's first job is finding the angle — not summarizing the topic.

2. **Twitter threads tell stories. LinkedIn posts share insights.** The same underlying content produces different structures for each platform. The thread moves — it has a beginning, middle, and turn. The LinkedIn post is more static: here's an observation, here's what I did about it, here's the takeaway. Different formats, different rhythms, different readers.

3. **Specificity is the difference between content that performs and content that doesn't.** "I was wasting food" is forgettable. "30-40% in the trash by Thursday" stops people. Numbers, timeframes, specific situations — these signal that a real person had a real experience. The AI-sounding version rounds all the edges off. The high-performing version keeps the jagged specifics.

4. **The hook tweet carries the thread.** In both examples, the hook tweet states the counterintuitive conclusion first — not the setup, not the backstory, the result. The thread then exists to explain how that result happened. This is the opposite of how most people write threads ("here's a topic I find interesting... here are some thoughts about it... here's a conclusion"). Lead with the conclusion, spend the rest of the thread justifying it.
